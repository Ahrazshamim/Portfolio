Multi-Source Geolocation System

Every connected vehicle needs to answer one question reliably: where is it? The hard part isn't getting a GPS fix — it's still answering when there's no sky, no cell tower, or the customer bought last year's hardware. I authored the PRD that made that answer consistent across all of it.

The problem

Our IoT module reports vehicle position in its heartbeat. That position was GNSS-only, which works beautifully right up until it doesn't: underground parking, dense urban canyons, a scooter parked inside a shop, a cold start with no almanac. In those cases the device reported nothing useful, and the customer-facing map showed a vehicle frozen at its last known point — indistinguishable, from the outside, from a vehicle that hadn't moved.

There was a second problem underneath it. We ship two hardware generations in the field simultaneously — v1.x and vB.x, on the EC200U-CNAA modem family — with different radio capabilities. Any feature that assumes the newer radio either breaks on older units or forces us to fork the firmware. Neither is acceptable on a product line where the same team maintains both.

The actual product question

Not "how do we make GPS better." It was: how do we produce a position, with an honest confidence attached, under every condition our hardware might be in — from one codebase? Framing it that way is what made graceful degradation a requirement instead of an afterthought.

The approach: collect on device, resolve on backend

The central architectural decision in the PRD was a clean split of responsibility. Firmware collects raw radio observations. The backend resolves them into a coordinate. The device never tries to be clever about which source "wins."

This was deliberate and it was the decision I defended hardest. Resolution logic changes often — you swap geolocation API providers, you tune weighting, you fix a bad tower database. Collection logic changes rarely. Putting resolution on the device would have meant a firmware campaign every time we wanted to improve accuracy; putting it on the backend means a deploy. It also let firmware and backend teams work in parallel against a contract rather than serially against each other.

Source hierarchy & graceful degradation

The three sources

SourceRoleWhat the PRD specified
GNSS Primary Multi-constellation (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) for fix availability; A-GNSS over SUPL to collapse time-to-first-fix on cold start; SBAS/GAGAN augmentation targeting sub-3 m accuracy in India.
WiFi Urban / indoor fallback Duty-cycled AP scanning, remotely configurable: trigger condition, scan duration, period, and max-AP count. Scanning is the expensive part, so every knob is tunable in the field rather than baked into a build.
Cellular Last-resort fallback Single Cell-ID for a coarse position, plus multilateration across neighbour cells (CEL_MULTI) when enough towers are visible. Always available if the device is online at all — which is the point.

The business case

This wasn't sold internally as an accuracy improvement. It was sold as removing a failure mode: location is only valuable if it never goes dark. A tracking product that fails in basements and tunnels isn't a tracking product — it's a fair-weather convenience, and fleet operators had learned not to rely on it.

There was a second, subtler problem the PRD had to solve: the last-metre problem. Even with a good position, a rider standing in a large parking lot still can't tell which of forty identical scooters is theirs. So the scope included a close-range BLE "find my vehicle" capability — and deliberately covered both Apple's Find My and Google's Find My Device networks, scoped around each ecosystem's distinct protocol and privacy model, so the feature is platform-agnostic to the end user rather than locking half the fleet out.

Decisions I'm glad I made

1. Capability-aware requirements, not version-specific ones

Rather than writing "on vB.x, do X," the PRD expresses requirements against capabilities the device reports about itself. A unit that can't do a given scan type simply omits that field; the backend sees the omission and falls through to the next source. One firmware codebase, both hardware generations, and — critically — a device that degrades visibly rather than failing silently. Silent failure was the outcome I was most concerned about, because it's the one that reaches the customer as "your product is wrong" instead of "your product is uncertain."

2. Positions carry their own uncertainty

Every reported position is labelled with confidence — CEP68 and HDOP. This sounds like a technical detail and is actually a product feature: it lets downstream consumers (geofencing, theft alerts, trip reconstruction) make different decisions with a 5-metre fix versus a 900-metre cell-tower estimate. Without it, every consumer has to guess, and they all guess differently.

3. Everything tunable over MQTT, persisted in NV memory

Scan parameters are remotely configurable over MQTT and stored in non-volatile memory so they survive reboot. This turns accuracy tuning into an operational lever instead of a release. When a specific customer's fleet operates somewhere with unusual radio conditions, we adjust their fleet — we don't ship a firmware campaign to everyone.

4. Power budget as a hard interlock

WiFi scanning costs current, and these devices sit in PSM (Power Saving Mode) on a battery that also has to start a vehicle. The PRD makes the power budget an explicit interlock: scan configuration cannot be set to values that violate sleep-mode current limits. This is the requirement that most obviously came from having written firmware — a purely product-side PRD would have specified the scan behaviour and discovered the brownout in the field.

Risk & failure-mode matrix

The section of the PRD I consider the most valuable wasn't the requirements — it was the failure-mode matrix. Each row is a plausible, well-intentioned change by one team that silently breaks another, with a named mitigation and a sign-off owner.

ChangeSilent consequenceMitigation
Firmware drops a cell field to save payload Backend's last-resort fallback stops working — but only for devices with no GNSS and no WiFi, so it never shows up in normal testing Cell fields declared mandatory in the contract; backend rejects and alerts on missing fields rather than defaulting
WiFi scan window shortened for power AP list truncates, resolution accuracy quietly degrades in exactly the dense-urban case WiFi exists to serve Minimum viable AP count specified; scan duration changes require joint firmware + backend sign-off
Scan duty cycle raised for accuracy PSM current budget exceeded → brownout risk on parked vehicles Hard configuration bounds enforced in firmware; power budget stated as a non-negotiable constraint in the PRD
Backend swaps geolocation API provider Different accuracy characteristics and coverage, with no change on the device side to explain it Resolution isolated behind a backend interface; uncertainty labels make the shift measurable rather than anecdotal
Why write it in EARS

EARS ("the system shall… when… while…") forces every requirement to state its trigger and precondition. On a multi-source, multi-hardware feature that's not pedantry — it's the difference between a requirement that survives handoff to a firmware engineer and one that generates three Slack threads. It also made the capability-aware requirements expressible at all, since the precondition is the capability.

  • A position under every condition — GNSS where there's sky, WiFi indoors and in urban canyons, cellular everywhere else. No more frozen dots.
  • Sub-3 m accuracy target in India via multi-constellation GNSS with SBAS/GAGAN augmentation, and dramatically faster cold-start fixes through A-GNSS/SUPL.

  • Accuracy became an operational lever — tunable per fleet over MQTT, no firmware campaign required.
  • Two teams shipped in parallel against a documented contract, with the failure-mode matrix catching the cross-team breakages before they reached the field.

What I'd take to the next one

The reusable lesson isn't about radios. It's that on any system spanning two teams, the interface contract and the failure-mode matrix are the product artifacts that matter most. Requirements describe the happy path, which everyone already agrees on. The matrix describes the ways reasonable people break each other's work while acting in good faith — and writing it down is what turns a dependency into a managed one.
