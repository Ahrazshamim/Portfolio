Modular Battery Swapping Station

A commercial rider doesn't lose money charging. They lose money waiting. This is a ground-up, modular swapping station that turns hours of downtime into a two-minute pit stop. It was built AUTOSAR-compliant from nothing because no off-the-shelf reference existed, and it was delivered as six pilot units for client field testing.

Why this project existed

Charging time is the defining bottleneck for commercial EV fleets. A delivery rider or e-rickshaw driver is paid by trips completed, so every hour a vehicle sits charging is lost income for both the driver and the operator. Swapping solves that directly, but the infrastructure to do it safely and reliably did not exist off the shelf for the client's needs.

  • Downtime kills fleet economics. Commercial drivers are paid per trip; charging hours are revenue neither they nor the operator ever recover.
  • No reliable modular station existed. The client needed something built from the ground up: safe, automated, and standards-compliant. It could not be a lab prototype that works only when an engineer is standing next to it.
  • Safety is non-negotiable. Swapping involves high-energy batteries and physical actuators. Any unsafe state, such as a battery ejecting mid-charge or a door unlocking at the wrong moment, is a serious hazard and an instant deal-breaker for deployment.
Core thesis

Whoever builds safe, reliable swapping infrastructure captures the recurring energy-as-a-service layer of the EV economy, which is far stickier and higher-value than one-time hardware sales. The station is the entry ticket; the per-swap economics are the actual business.

How it works: the swap sequence

The station runs a strict, safety-gated state machine. Every transition has a defined fail-safe, so an unsafe state is physically impossible rather than merely discouraged. That distinction is what separates a deployable product from a demo.

Safety architecture

Three principles carried the whole design, and all three came from the same insight: on a high-energy system, safety cannot be a procedure people follow. It has to be a state the machine cannot enter.

  • Every actuator has an explicit fail-safe default. Loss of power, loss of comms or an unexpected reset all resolve to the same known-safe configuration rather than to whatever the last command happened to be.
  • Interlock conditions make unsafe transitions impossible. Ejecting a charging battery or unlocking mid-cycle isn't guarded against in software logic; it's gated at the transition itself.
  • Modular subsystems were built and tested in parallel against defined interfaces. Sequencing, actuator control, SoC display, and interlocks each developed independently, which is what made a three-month timeline survivable, with mechanical, electrical, and embedded teams working simultaneously.
Why model-based development earned its keep

Building the sequencing in MATLAB/Simulink/Stateflow meant the state machine was reviewable as a diagram by mechanical and client stakeholders who don't read Embedded C, and the same model generated MISRA-compliant code. On a safety-gated system, having one artefact that is simultaneously the specification, the review surface and the source of the implementation removes an entire class of spec-drift failures.

What I'd take to the next one

On safety-critical hardware, define the interfaces before the components. It looks like overhead in week one and it's the only reason three disciplines could build simultaneously against a fixed date. And the framing that made the AUTOSAR scope decision easy: compliance isn't a checkbox you satisfy at the end, it's a constraint you choose the MVP boundary around at the start. So pick the safety-critical core, commit to it fully, and defer everything that isn't.
