SAF Connect: Connecting Care with Technology

A family calling six hospitals to find one free ICU bed does not have a technology problem. It's an information problem that technology happens to solve. SAF Connect is the healthcare platform I founded around that gap: real-time visibility of critical hospital resources on one side, and an HMS that earns hospitals' data on the other.

The problem worth solving

The problem I identified as worth solving is the lack of real-time information on critical hospital resources. When a family needs an ICU bed, a ventilator or a child nursery place, there is no way to find out who has one free. They call hospitals one at a time, get inconsistent answers, and physically travel between facilities during an emergency.

It mirrors on the provider side. Hospitals, clinics and doctors' offices run on outdated systems, manual record-keeping and fragmented tools, and this produces inefficiency, data loss and inflated operating costs. Many hospitals have the facilities patients are hunting for; they're just invisible and often already occupied.

Validation before building

I didn't want a plausible problem, I wanted a measured one. In customer interviews, approximately 80% confirmed they were facing this problem. More usefully, they described how they currently cope: outdated systems, manual records, repetitive phone calls and in-person visits. Those workarounds became the real competitive set.

Problem validation & the status quo

The solution, in three parts

01

One integrated system

Hospital, clinic and doctor unified into a single, easy-to-use platform instead of four disconnected tools.

02

Real-time tracking

Live availability of ICU beds, ventilators, nursery facilities and doctor schedules, visible to patients before they travel.

03

Resource optimisation

Advanced tools for tracking and optimising utilisation, with analytics and AI integration for providers.

The strategic insight

Real-time patient-facing data only exists if hospitals enter it, and hospitals won't do data entry as charity. So the patient app can't be the product; the HMS is the product, and the real-time feed is its by-product. Hospitals adopt it to reduce their own admin burden, and patient visibility comes free. That two-sided sequencing decision shaped the entire roadmap.

Who I built it for

Two-sided marketplaces fail when you design for the side that's easier to imagine. I wrote both personas properly before scoping anything, because their needs conflict. Providers want less admin, patients want more transparency, and transparency is admin unless the system produces it automatically.

Value proposition canvas

I ran the canvas separately for each side. The exercise that mattered was writing the pains honestly, including the ones our own category creates.

The pricing decision

Every incumbent HMS prices by individual quotation. That's a deliberate friction. It means a sales call, a negotiation and no idea what you'll pay before you engage. A declared, freemium subscription is a genuine wedge for small clinics who would never survive that process, and it directly serves the pain we measured: existing tools are expensive and need a technical hire to operate.

Prioritisation & delivery

I scored the backlog with RICE in the PRD. In a large org RICE is mostly a negotiation tool; as a founder the Confidence column does the real work, because it forces you to separate what you've validated from what you personally find interesting. Several features I was attached to scored badly on confidence and got cut. That is precisely what the framework is for, given that nobody else was going to push back on my prioritisation.

I then managed the full SDLC through five pilot launches. Five rather than one was intentional: a single pilot tells you about one site's idiosyncrasies and gives you no way to distinguish a product problem from a local one. Each ran against KPIs defined before launch, because you cannot retroactively measure a pilot that has already ended.

What it taught me that the day job couldn't

Two things. First, scope discipline is a survival skill when nothing is externally constrained. With no customer deadline and no platform limits, the only thing stopping you shipping everything badly is a framework you impose on yourself. Second, instrument before you launch, always. The pilots that taught me most were the ones I'd set up to measure in advance; the insights I lost were the ones I hadn't thought to instrument. That habit came back to Vecmocon with me and made me considerably more rigorous about defining success metrics before a feature ships rather than after.
